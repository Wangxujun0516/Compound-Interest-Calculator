Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?
Compound interest is the interest calculated on both your initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. Unlike simple interest (which only earns on the principal), compound interest creates a snowball effect where your money grows exponentially over time. It's often called the "eighth wonder of the world" because of its powerful growth potential.
How does compounding frequency affect my returns?
The more frequently interest compounds, the faster your money grows. Daily compounding yields slightly more than monthly, which yields more than quarterly, which yields more than annual compounding. For example, $10,000 at 8% annual interest compounded daily earns about $81 more over 10 years than the same rate compounded annually. The difference becomes more significant with larger amounts and longer time horizons.
What is the Rule of 72?
The Rule of 72 is a simple mental math trick to estimate how long it takes your money to double at a given interest rate. Divide 72 by your annual interest rate. For example, at 12% interest: 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years to double your money. At 8%: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. It's a useful approximation for quick planning, though actual results depend on compounding frequency and regular contributions.
Does it matter when I make deposits (beginning vs. end of month)?
Yes! Deposits made at the beginning of each period have more time to earn interest, which increases your total returns. Over long periods, this timing difference can be significant. For instance, with a $1,000 monthly deposit at 12% annual interest over 30 years, beginning-of-month deposits could earn roughly $50,000+ more than end-of-month deposits due to the extra compounding time.
What is the difference between nominal and effective annual rate?
The nominal annual rate is the stated interest rate before accounting for compounding. The effective annual rate (EAR) reflects the actual annual return after compounding is factored in. For example, a 12% nominal rate compounded monthly gives an EAR of about 12.68%. This calculator shows the effective annual rate so you can compare different compounding frequencies on an apples-to-apples basis.
How do taxes and inflation affect my investment returns?
Taxes reduce your net returns — you typically pay capital gains tax on investment earnings. Inflation erodes purchasing power over time. If your investment earns 8% but inflation is 3%, your real return is only about 5%. For accurate long-term planning, consider using a lower rate to account for these factors, or consult with a financial advisor who can model your specific tax situation.
What is a realistic long-term investment return?
Historical averages vary by asset class: the S&P 500 has averaged about 10% annually before inflation (roughly 7% after inflation) over the long term. Bonds typically return 3-5%. A balanced portfolio might return 6-8%. Be cautious with assumptions above 12% — higher potential returns come with higher risk. This calculator is a planning tool; actual returns will vary and past performance doesn't guarantee future results.
Should I invest a lump sum or use dollar-cost averaging?
Historically, lump sum investing has outperformed dollar-cost averaging (DCA) about two-thirds of the time because markets tend to go up over time. However, DCA can reduce psychological stress and the risk of investing a large amount right before a market downturn. The best approach depends on your risk tolerance, time horizon, and personal situation. Many advisors recommend lump sum for long-term investors with high risk tolerance.
How do withdrawals affect my compounding growth?
Withdrawals reduce both your principal and future compounding potential. Every dollar withdrawn is a dollar that would have earned future returns. This is why early withdrawals from retirement accounts can be so costly — you lose both the money withdrawn AND all the future growth it would have generated. The advanced mode lets you model the impact of periodic withdrawals so you can plan accordingly.
What is the best way to use this calculator?
Start with realistic assumptions based on your actual financial situation. Use the basic mode for simple projections, and the advanced mode to model multiple income streams or planned withdrawals. Save different scenarios to compare strategies. Most importantly, remember that all projections are estimates — use this tool for planning and education, not as a guarantee of future returns. Always consult a qualified financial advisor for personalized advice.
